Unit 1 — Introduction to Security
1.1 Social Engineering
What is Social Engineering?
Social engineering is the use of psychological manipulation to trick people into revealing information, taking an action, or giving an attacker access to something.
Instead of attacking a computer directly, an attacker may attack the person using the computer.
This is important because even a highly secure system can be compromised if someone is tricked into giving away their credentials or access.
Why does social engineering work?
Social engineering attacks often take advantage of normal human behaviors and emotions.
Attackers may try to create:
•	Urgency — “You must act right now.” 
•	Fear — “Your account will be deleted.” 
•	Authority — “This is your school administrator.” 
•	Curiosity — “Look at this secret document.” 
•	Trust — pretending to be someone the victim knows. 
•	Greed — promising money, prizes, or other rewards. 
•	Helpfulness — convincing someone that they are helping another person. 
The attacker wants the victim to react instead of carefully thinking about the request.
________________________________________
Phishing
Phishing is a social engineering attack in which an attacker uses a fake message to trick someone into providing information or performing an action.
Phishing can happen through:
•	Email 
•	Text messages 
•	Social media 
•	Messaging applications 
•	Fake websites 
For example, you might receive a message claiming that your school account has a problem and asking you to click a link and sign in.
The link may lead to a fake login page designed to collect your username and password.
Signs of phishing
Look for:
•	Unexpected messages 
•	Urgent or threatening language 
•	Suspicious links 
•	Strange sender addresses 
•	Requests for passwords or sensitive information 
•	Spelling or grammar that seems unusual 
•	Unexpected attachments 
•	Messages that seem too good to be true 
Important: A message can look professional and still be malicious.
________________________________________
Spear Phishing
Spear phishing is a phishing attack specifically targeted at a particular person or organization.
Instead of sending the same message to thousands of people, the attacker researches the target and creates a more believable message.
For example:
An attacker discovers that a student is part of the school's robotics club. The attacker sends an email pretending to be the club advisor and asks the student to open a document.
The more information an attacker knows about a target, the more convincing the attack can become.
________________________________________
Whaling
Whaling is a type of targeted phishing aimed at an important or high-value individual.
Targets may include:
•	Executives 
•	School administrators 
•	Business leaders 
•	Financial officers 
•	Other people with significant authority or access 
The goal is often to convince the target to authorize something valuable or provide access to sensitive information.
Remember:
Phishing → broad targeting
Spear phishing → specific target
Whaling → high-value individual
These categories can overlap because whaling is essentially a highly targeted form of phishing.
________________________________________
Smishing
Smishing is phishing through SMS/text messages.
Example:
“Your package could not be delivered. Confirm your information here.”
The message may contain a malicious link.
SMishing = SMS + phishing
________________________________________
Vishing
Vishing is phishing through voice communication, such as a phone call.
An attacker may pretend to be:
•	A bank employee 
•	A technical-support employee 
•	A government employee 
•	A school administrator 
The attacker may attempt to convince the victim to reveal information or perform an action.
Vishing = Voice + phishing
________________________________________
Pretexting
Pretexting occurs when an attacker creates a false story or identity to convince someone to provide information.
The attacker establishes a believable pretext, or reason for contacting the victim.
For example:
Someone contacts a school's technology department pretending to be a new employee who needs access to an account.
The attacker is relying on the victim believing the story.
________________________________________
Baiting
Baiting uses something appealing to trick someone into taking an unsafe action.
The “bait” could be:
•	A free item 
•	A fake prize 
•	An interesting file 
•	A tempting download 
•	A message promising something valuable 
The goal is to make the victim curious enough to interact with the bait.
________________________________________
Tailgating
Tailgating is a physical form of social engineering.
An unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted area.
For example:
An employee opens a secure door, and someone follows behind them without using their own access card.
The attacker is taking advantage of social politeness and trust.
________________________________________
Social Engineering: What You Should Remember
The most important idea is:
Security is not only about protecting computers. People are part of the security system too.
When evaluating a suspicious message, ask:
1.	Who sent it? 
2.	Was I expecting it? 
3.	What is the person asking me to do? 
4.	Is the request urgent or threatening? 
5.	Is sensitive information being requested? 
6.	Can I verify the request another way? 
AP Skill: Analyze Risk
When given a social engineering scenario, identify:
Asset → Threat → Vulnerability → Potential Impact
Example:
Asset: Student's school account
Threat: Phishing attacker
Vulnerability: Student trusts a fake login message
Impact: Attacker gains access to the account
AP Skill: Mitigate Risk
Possible mitigations include:
•	Verify unexpected requests 
•	Avoid clicking suspicious links 
•	Use strong authentication 
•	Use multifactor authentication 
•	Report suspicious messages 
•	Limit the information publicly available about yourself 
•	Follow organizational security policies

1.2 Suspicious Website Logins & Password Attacks
Authentication
Authentication is the process of verifying that someone is who they claim to be.
A website might authenticate you using:
•	Something you know — a password 
•	Something you have — a security key or phone 
•	Something you are — a biometric characteristic 
A username identifies an account.
A password helps authenticate the person attempting to access that account.
________________________________________
Why Passwords Matter
Passwords are often the first barrier protecting an account.
If an attacker obtains your password, they may be able to access:
•	Personal information 
•	Messages 
•	Files 
•	Financial information 
•	School accounts 
•	Other connected services 
A password therefore represents an important security control.
________________________________________
Brute-Force Attacks
A brute-force attack attempts to discover a password by systematically trying many possible passwords.
The basic idea is simple:
Try possible passwords until the correct one is found.
The difficulty of a brute-force attack depends on factors such as:
•	Password length 
•	Password complexity 
•	Number of possible combinations 
•	Login protections 
•	Whether the attacker can make unlimited attempts 
Defending against brute force
Systems can make brute-force attacks harder through:
•	Strong passwords 
•	Rate limiting 
•	Account lockouts 
•	Multifactor authentication 
•	Monitoring failed login attempts 
________________________________________
Password Spraying
Password spraying is different from traditional brute force.
Instead of trying many passwords against one account, an attacker may try a small number of commonly used passwords against many accounts.
This can help attackers avoid triggering protections that detect repeated attempts against a single account.
The important distinction is:
Brute force: many password attempts → one account
Password spraying: a few common passwords → many accounts
________________________________________
Credential Stuffing
Credential stuffing uses stolen username/password combinations obtained from previous data breaches.
Attackers rely on people reusing passwords across multiple websites.
For example:
A password is stolen from Website A.
The attacker tries the same username and password on Website B.
If the person reused the password, the second account may also be compromised.
Why password reuse is dangerous
If every account has the same password, one compromised account can potentially lead to multiple compromised accounts.
Using different passwords for different accounts limits the damage from a single breach.
________________________________________
Rainbow Tables
A rainbow table is a precomputed collection used to help attackers recover passwords from certain types of password hashes.
You don't need to know the mathematics behind rainbow tables yet.
The important idea is:
Attackers can sometimes use precomputed information to make recovering weak passwords from stolen password data faster.
This is one reason modern systems use techniques such as salting passwords before hashing them.
Hashing vs. Encryption
These should not be confused.
Encryption is designed so information can be decrypted back into its original form using the appropriate key.
Hashing is designed as a one-way transformation.
Passwords should generally be stored using appropriate password-hashing techniques rather than being stored as plain text.
________________________________________
Why You Shouldn't Reuse Passwords
Imagine you use the same password for:
•	Your gaming account 
•	Your email 
•	Your school account 
•	Your social media 
If one service experiences a breach and your password is exposed, an attacker could attempt to use those credentials elsewhere.
This is called credential stuffing.
Better practice
Use a unique password or passphrase for each important account.
A password manager can help users generate and remember unique passwords.
________________________________________
Should You Change Your Password Regularly?
Changing passwords can be useful when there is a reason to believe a password has been:
•	Stolen 
•	Exposed 
•	Shared 
•	Compromised 
•	Used on a breached service 
However, constantly changing a password for no reason is not necessarily the most important security improvement.
A better approach is to focus on:
•	Unique passwords 
•	Strong passwords/passphrases 
•	Multifactor authentication 
•	Not sharing passwords 
•	Changing compromised passwords promptly 
________________________________________
Suspicious Login Activity
Cybersecurity isn't just about creating a strong password.
You should also be able to detect suspicious authentication activity.
Examples include:
•	Repeated failed login attempts 
•	A login from an unexpected location 
•	A login at an unusual time 
•	An unfamiliar device 
•	Password-reset requests you didn't make 
•	Unexpected security notifications 
Logs can provide evidence that something suspicious may be happening.
The AP framework uses scenarios involving login records and asks students to identify patterns and suspicious entries. 
________________________________________
What You Should Remember
Attack types
Attack	Basic idea
Brute force	Try many possible passwords
Password spraying	Try common passwords against many accounts
Credential stuffing	Try stolen credentials on other services
Rainbow tables	Use precomputed information to help recover certain password hashes
Good password security
Remember:
Unique + Strong + Protected + MFA
A strong authentication system makes it harder for an attacker to guess, steal, or reuse credentials.

1.3 Security Practices & Public Networks
This section should introduce students to a bigger idea:
Good cybersecurity isn't one tool. It is multiple security controls working together.
This connects directly to defense in depth, which is a major idea in cybersecurity. College Board describes the course as emphasizing defense-in-depth strategies across different types of systems and environments. 
________________________________________
Public Wi-Fi
Public Wi-Fi can be convenient, but students should understand that connecting to a network means trusting the network to some degree.
Examples include:
•	Coffee shops 
•	Airports 
•	Hotels 
•	Libraries 
•	Schools 
•	Shopping centers 
Risks of public networks
Potential risks include:
•	Connecting to a malicious or fake network 
•	Unauthorized monitoring of network traffic 
•	Exposure of sensitive information 
•	Attacks against poorly secured devices 
•	Account compromise 
A particularly important warning sign is a network that looks legitimate but isn't actually operated by the organization it claims to represent.
________________________________________
Evil Twin Networks
An evil twin is a fake Wi-Fi network designed to imitate a legitimate network.
For example, suppose a coffee shop normally provides:
CoffeeShop_WiFi
An attacker could create a nearby network with a similar name.
A user might accidentally connect to the attacker's network.
Protect yourself
Before connecting:
•	Verify the network name 
•	Ask an employee for the correct network 
•	Avoid performing highly sensitive activities on untrusted networks 
•	Use encrypted connections 
•	Keep your device and security software updated 
________________________________________
Least Privilege
Least privilege is the principle that users and systems should receive only the permissions they need to perform their job or task—and no more.
For example:
A student who only needs to read a file shouldn't automatically have permission to delete it.
An employee who only needs access to one department's data shouldn't automatically have access to the entire company's database.
Why least privilege matters
Suppose an attacker compromises an account.
If that account has administrator privileges, the attacker may have much more control.
If the account has limited privileges, the potential damage can be reduced.
So:
Less access → less potential damage
Least privilege is therefore both a security and risk-management concept.
________________________________________
Defense in Depth
Defense in depth means using multiple security controls so that one failed control does not automatically result in a complete compromise.
For example:
Password
↓
Multifactor authentication
↓
Firewall
↓
Endpoint protection
↓
Access controls
↓
Monitoring and logging
Each control provides another opportunity to stop or detect an attack.
The AP course itself emphasizes defense-in-depth strategies. 
________________________________________
The Security Onion
The security onion is an analogy for defense in depth.
Imagine an onion with multiple layers.
An attacker attempting to reach the center must get through multiple layers of protection.
The important lesson is:
One security control should not be expected to protect everything.
________________________________________
The Security Artichoke
The security artichoke is another analogy for layered security.
Unlike the onion, where an attacker might need to work through every layer, an attacker may be able to compromise individual “leaves” or exposed parts of a larger environment and use those weaknesses to move toward more valuable information.
The analogy is useful because modern environments can contain many interconnected devices, accounts, applications, and services. 
Think about it this way:
Security Onion
“There are many layers between the attacker and the important data.”
Security Artichoke
“There are many connected pieces, and compromising one exposed piece may provide a path toward something more valuable.”
________________________________________
Other Important Security Practices
Multifactor Authentication
Multifactor authentication (MFA) requires more than one type of authentication factor.
For example:
Password + code from your phone
Even if an attacker obtains the password, they may still be unable to access the account.
________________________________________
Patching and Updates
Software can contain vulnerabilities.
Patching means applying updates that fix bugs or security vulnerabilities.
Keeping software updated reduces the number of known vulnerabilities attackers can exploit.
________________________________________
Secure Configuration
A system should be configured with security in mind.
Examples include:
•	Disable unnecessary services 
•	Remove unnecessary accounts 
•	Change default credentials 
•	Restrict permissions 
•	Enable appropriate security controls 
________________________________________
Backups
A backup is a separate copy of important data.
Backups can help organizations recover from incidents such as:
•	Hardware failure 
•	Accidental deletion 
•	Malware 
•	Ransomware 
•	Other forms of data loss 
A backup is most useful when it is protected from the same incident that affects the original data.

Unit 1.4 What Students Should Be Able to Do
By the end of 1.1–1.3, students should be able to:
1. Recognize social engineering
Students should be able to identify:
•	Phishing 
•	Spear phishing 
•	Whaling 
•	Smishing 
•	Vishing 
•	Pretexting 
•	Baiting 
•	Tailgating 
And explain what makes a situation suspicious.
2. Analyze a cybersecurity risk
Given a scenario, students should be able to identify:
Asset → Threat → Vulnerability → Impact
3. Recognize basic password attacks
Students should understand the difference between:
•	Brute force 
•	Password spraying 
•	Credential stuffing 
•	Rainbow tables 
They should also understand why password reuse creates additional risk.
4. Recognize suspicious authentication activity
Students should be able to look at basic login information and identify unusual patterns.
5. Explain least privilege
Students should understand why giving users unnecessary permissions increases risk.
6. Explain defense in depth
Students should understand why organizations use multiple security controls instead of relying on one defense.
7. Understand public-network risks
Students should recognize risks associated with public Wi-Fi and understand the basic idea behind attacks such as evil-twin networks.
