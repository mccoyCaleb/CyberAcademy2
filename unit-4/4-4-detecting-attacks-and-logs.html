4.4 • Detecting attacks and logs

Detecting Attacks and Analyzing Logs

Prevention is important, but defenders also need ways to identify suspicious activity after it begins. Logs and indicators of compromise help analysts determine whether a device or account may have been affected.

ProgressMonitoring and response
Overview 4.1 4.2 4.3 4.4
Big idea

Logs turn activity into evidence.

Logs record what happened on devices, applications, and networks. They help analysts answer what happened, when it happened, where it happened, and what may have been affected.

Types of logs

System logs record details about the operating system, services, hardware, and configuration changes. They can show when a service stops, a program starts, or a system setting changes unexpectedly.

Authentication logs record logins, failed logins, and sign-in events. A pattern of repeated failed login attempts from one source is often a warning sign.

Application logs show what a program or service is doing. They can reveal configuration changes, unexpected errors, unusual actions, or suspicious administrative tasks.

Network and firewall logs capture connections, blocked traffic, and unusual communication attempts. These logs help analysts see whether a host is contacting suspicious addresses or receiving repeated connection attempts.

Reading suspicious patterns

Example timeline

2026-10-02 09:14:02 Login failed: user=alex

2026-10-02 09:14:08 Login failed: user=alex

2026-10-02 09:14:15 Login failed: user=alex

2026-10-02 09:14:21 Login failed: user=alex

2026-10-02 09:14:29 Login success: user=alex

Several failed attempts followed by a success could indicate password guessing, a user entering the wrong password, or an attacker testing credentials. The analyst should look for source IP, unusual timing, the account involved, and any subsequent actions.

IoCs

Indicators of compromise

An indicator of compromise (IoC) is evidence suggesting system or account compromise. Examples include:

  • Many failed logins against one account
  • Unexpected new admin accounts
  • Unfamiliar processes running on a device
  • Unauthorized system changes
  • Connections to suspicious external servers

IoCs do not automatically prove an attack succeeded, but they are strong reasons to investigate further.

Investigation workflow

When analyzing suspicious activity, investigators try to answer:

  • What happened? Identify the recorded event.
  • When did it happen? Use timestamps to build a timeline.
  • Where did it happen? Identify the device, account, or source involved.
  • What may have been affected? Determine which accounts, files, or systems were involved.

Checklist

Remember

  • Logs are evidence, not assumptions.
  • Patterns matter more than single events.
  • One failed login may be normal; repeated failures deserve investigation.
  • Analysts combine evidence from several systems before concluding what happened.
