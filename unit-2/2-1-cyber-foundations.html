2.1 • Cyber Foundations

Cyber Foundations

This section covers the human layer of cybersecurity, the adversaries behind attacks, and the structured process by which attackers gather information, gain access, move through a system, and try to avoid detection.

ProgressHuman factors
Overview 2.1 2.2 2.3 2.4 2.5
Big idea

Cybersecurity is not only technical.

Attackers often do not need to defeat technology directly. They may manipulate a person into revealing data, approving access, or allowing entry into a protected space. In fact, social engineering can be more effective than a direct technical exploit because it targets normal human decision-making.

Social engineering

Manipulating behavior

Attackers exploit trust, authority, urgency, fear, familiarity, scarcity, and group pressure to make a target act without full evaluation.

Threat actors

Motivation matters

Script kiddies, hacktivists, insiders, cyberterrorists, and transnational criminal organizations all attack for different reasons and with different capabilities.

Cyberattack phases

Research to evasion

Attackers often move through reconnaissance, initial access, persistence, lateral movement, taking action, and evasion in a sequence designed to avoid detection.

Social engineering

Social engineering is the use of psychological or social techniques to manipulate a person into taking an action that benefits an adversary. Instead of breaking into a computer directly, an attacker may convince a person to open a malicious file, click a link, reveal a password, share personal information, approve a login request, let someone in, transfer money, or ignore a warning.

Pretexting is when an attacker creates a believable story or reason for a request. The attacker invents a pretext that makes the interaction sound legitimate.

Example: An attacker contacts an employee and claims to work in IT, says there is a problem with the employee’s account, and asks for information so the account can be fixed.

Student reminder: Pretexting = a believable story.

Authority occurs when an attacker pretends to be someone with power, status, expertise, or influence.

Examples include a principal, teacher, manager, administrator, police officer, or IT employee. People often comply faster when they believe the request comes from someone important.

Student reminder: Authority = pressure created by status or power.

Intimidation uses fear or threats of negative consequences. The attacker wants the target to become worried enough that they stop evaluating whether the request is legitimate.

Example: “If you don’t complete this form immediately, your account will be suspended.”

Student reminder: Intimidation = fear of what will happen if you do not comply.

Consensus creates social pressure by making a person believe that others have already done something.

Example: “Everyone else in your class has already completed this form. You’re the only one who hasn’t.”

Student reminder: Consensus = pressure from the group.

Scarcity makes something seem limited or available for only a brief time. The attacker wants the target to believe the opportunity will disappear if they wait.

Warning signs include “Only 2 spots left,” “Last chance,” “Offer expires today,” and “You must act before the timer ends.”

Student reminder: Scarcity = limited opportunity.

Familiarity occurs when an attacker pretends to be, know, or have a relationship with someone the target already trusts.

Example: An attacker pretends to be a student’s friend and asks for a file or password. Attackers may also mimic the communication style of someone familiar.

Student reminder: Familiarity = trust based on something or someone known.

Urgency creates pressure to act immediately. It is especially powerful when combined with another tactic.

Example: “Your account will be deleted in 10 minutes unless you verify it.”

Authority + Urgency: “I am from IT, and your account will be locked in five minutes.”

Student reminder: Urgency = pressure to act quickly.

Social engineering attack types

Phishing is a social engineering attack that tries to trick someone through a message, often an email, into taking an unsafe action.

Possible goals include clicking a malicious link, opening an attachment, providing credentials, revealing financial information, or installing malware.

Spear phishing is phishing targeted at a specific person or organization rather than mass sent. The attacker may use information about the target to make the message more believable.

Whaling is a targeted phishing attack aimed at a high-value individual, such as an executive or senior decision-maker. The goal is often to exploit authority, access, or the ability to approve important actions.

Vishing is voice-based phishing, usually over a phone call or voice communication.

Smishing is phishing sent through text messages or mobile messaging.

Baiting uses something attractive or tempting to encourage unsafe behavior, such as a free file, reward, or device.

Quid pro quo offers something in exchange for the target’s action or information. For example, an attacker may claim to provide technical help in exchange for credentials.

Types of adversaries

Not all attackers are the same

Knowing who the adversary is can help defenders predict what they may attempt and what kinds of controls may be necessary.

  • Script kiddies: relatively low-skilled attackers who use tools created by others. They may be motivated by curiosity, recognition, entertainment, or greed.
  • Hacktivists: motivated by social, political, ideological, or personal beliefs; their main goal is often to support a cause or protest an organization.
  • Insider adversaries: people with legitimate access such as employees, contractors, or former employees whose access remains active. Their motivations may include greed, revenge, pressure, or helping another person.
  • Cyberterrorists: motivated by political or ideological causes and may target critical infrastructure to create widespread disruption.
  • Transnational criminal organizations: primarily seek financial gain and may deploy ransomware, steal credentials, steal personal data, or sell stolen information.

Cyberattack phases

Attack lifecycle

Six phases

Reconnaissance ↓ Initial access ↓ Persistence ↓ Lateral movement ↓ Taking action ↓ Evading detection

Reconnaissance is research and information gathering using public sources, profiles, websites, and other available data. Initial access is how the attacker gets in. Persistence is how they maintain access over time. Lateral movement means moving through the environment toward more valuable systems or privileges. Taking action means collecting, stealing, modifying, damaging, or exfiltrating data. Evading detection means hiding tracks or deleting evidence.

Reconnaissance is the process of gathering information about a target before or during the attack. This can include publicly available information, employee roles, technology responsibilities, department names, and social media posts.

OSINT refers to open-source intelligence, or information that is publicly available.

Initial access happens when the attacker establishes an entry point. Possible methods include social engineering, compromised credentials, weak credentials, or the exploitation of a vulnerability.

Persistence is the ability to maintain access over time. The attacker may use command and control infrastructure, remote access trojans, or rootkits to regain access later.

Lateral movement occurs when the attacker moves from one compromised account or system to others within the environment. The attacker may look for other user accounts, more valuable systems, or accounts with greater privileges.

Privilege escalation means gaining a higher level of access than the attacker originally had.

Taking action is when the attacker accomplishes the actual objective: collecting, stealing, destroying, modifying, or disrupting data or services.

Exfiltration means moving stolen data out of the target environment.

Evading detection is when attackers try to hide evidence of what they have done by removing log files, deleting malware, or editing records.

AP clue: If the attacker is covering tracks or trying not to be noticed, think evading detection.

Risk model

Threat + vulnerability + asset

Risk occurs when a threat can exploit a vulnerability and compromise an asset. A useful model is: Threat + Vulnerability → Risk to an Asset.

  • Asset: Something valuable that needs protection, such as money, data, digital infrastructure, reputation, or physical property.
  • Threat: A thing capable of causing harm, such as an attacker, malware, an insider, a natural disaster, or equipment failure.
  • Vulnerability: A weakness or flaw that could allow an asset to be compromised, such as weak authentication, unrestricted access, or an unlocked door.

Risk = likelihood + impact

Risk is not defined only by the existence of a threat. It also depends on whether a vulnerability is present, whether the asset is valuable, and how damaging the outcome would be.

  • Likelihood: How likely is it that the exploit or attack will occur?
  • Impact: How much damage could the compromise cause to the asset, organization, or people involved?
  • Exposure: How exposed is the asset to the threat if the vulnerability remains unaddressed?

A weakly protected file server with sensitive student records may have higher risk than a public website with no sensitive data, even if both are connected to the same network.

An attacker says, “Your manager asked me to get this file from you.” Which tactic is being used?

Correct: This is authority. The attacker is using the perceived power or status of the manager to increase compliance.
