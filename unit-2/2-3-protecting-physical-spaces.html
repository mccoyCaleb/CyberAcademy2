2.3 • Protecting Physical Spaces

Protecting Physical Spaces

Once a vulnerability is identified, the next question is: what control should be used to reduce the risk? Strong physical security depends on people, policies, physical barriers, and layered protections that work together.

Big idea

Good defenses match the vulnerability.

A strong security decision does not just name a control. It explains why that control fits the specific weakness in the environment. A control is most useful when it prevents, detects, or corrects the scenario being considered.

People

Security awareness

Employees are part of the security system. Training helps them recognize phishing, social engineering, suspicious requests, and unauthorized physical access.

Policies

Workstation security

Rules such as locking devices, using clean desks, protecting screens, and controlling device placement reduce accidental exposure and theft.

Physical controls

Access and power protection

Locks, cameras, bollards, guards, UPS devices, and surge protectors are physical controls that help prevent access, detect issues, and reduce disruption.

Risk mitigation and decision-making

Once a risk has been identified and assessed, an organization chooses among four strategies: avoidance, transference, mitigation, and acceptance.

Risk avoidance means stopping the activity that creates the risk. If a technology or task is too risky, the organization removes it entirely.

Key phrase: Avoid = stop the risky activity.

Risk transference means shifting some of the burden or consequences to another entity, such as an insurance company or a contractor.

The original organization may still experience some effects, but some responsibilities or costs are shifted elsewhere.

Risk mitigation means implementing controls to reduce the likelihood or impact of a risk. This is usually the most common response to a security weakness.

Examples include installing locks, adding authentication, encrypting data, training employees, installing cameras, creating backups, and improving access controls.

Important idea: Mitigation reduces the risk. It does not necessarily eliminate it.

Risk acceptance means the organization knowingly decides to live with the risk because the likelihood or impact is low, the cost of mitigation is not worth it, or the activity is necessary.

Accepting risk does not mean the risk is gone. It means the organization has chosen not to reduce it further.

Residual risk is the risk that remains after controls and other risk-management actions have been applied. No organization can guarantee zero risk.

Organizations aim to reduce risk to a level they can manage.

Security controls

The CIA triad describes the main goals of information security: confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

Confidentiality = information is only available to those authorized to access it. Integrity = information remains accurate and trustworthy. Availability = authorized users can access systems and data when needed.

Memory trick: Confidentiality = private; Integrity = correct; Availability = accessible when needed.

Physical controls protect physical spaces, equipment, and people. Examples include locks, fences, cameras, security guards, bollards, and access-controlled doors.

Technical controls use technology such as firewalls, encryption, anti-malware software, and authentication systems.

Managerial controls are policies, procedures, training, access reviews, and incident response plans used to guide behavior and decision-making.

Preventative controls try to stop a problem before it happens. Examples include locks, authentication, encryption, and barriers.

Detective controls help identify attacks or suspicious activity. Examples include cameras, motion sensors, IDS, and SIEM systems.

Corrective controls help restore systems after an event. These include system recovery, backups, patching, and repairing damaged equipment.

This is a common AP confusion point. Ask two separate questions:

1. What kind of control is it? Physical, technical, or managerial.
2. What does it do? Preventative, detective, or corrective.

Example: A security camera is a physical control and a detective control. Encryption is a technical control and often preventative. A security policy is a managerial control.

Defense in depth uses multiple layers of security to protect systems and data. If one control fails, another may still stop, detect, or reduce the attack.

The AP framework identifies six possible layers: human, physical, network, device, application, and data.

Key idea: Defense in depth is not about making one defense stronger. It is about building multiple barriers.

Practical protection measures

Controls that reduce physical risk

  • Security awareness training: teaches employees what suspicious behavior looks like and how to respond.
  • Lock your device: prevents unauthorized use when the computer is left unattended.
  • Clean desk policy: reduces exposure of sensitive documents and printed data.
  • Privacy screens: reduce visual disclosure in open areas or shared spaces.
  • Surge protectors and UPS devices: reduce equipment damage and support availability when power is interrupted.
  • Locks, guards, cameras, and access-controlled doors: provide physical barriers that reduce unauthorized entry.

A school discovers that anyone can walk into a room containing a server and no one monitors it. What response best matches the risk?

Correct: This is risk mitigation. The school is reducing the likelihood and impact of unauthorized physical access.

  • The best response often depends on the vulnerability and the asset at risk.
  • Controls can protect confidentiality, integrity, and availability.
  • Physical security is a foundational layer in cybersecurity.
