1.1 • Social Engineering

Social Engineering

Social engineering is the use of psychological manipulation to trick people into revealing information, taking an action, or giving an attacker access to something.

Big idea

Security is not only about protecting computers.

Instead of attacking a computer directly, an attacker may attack the person using the computer. This is important because even a highly secure system can be compromised if someone is tricked into giving away credentials or access.

Why it works

Normal human behavior

Attackers often exploit emotions such as urgency, fear, trust, curiosity, greed, and helpfulness.

Common tricks

Pressure and deception

Attackers may say: “You must act right now,” or pretend to be a school administrator, friend, or trusted contact.

What to do

Pause and verify

Ask: Who sent it? Was I expecting it? What are they asking me to do? Can I verify the request another way?

Types of social engineering

Phishing is a fake message used to trick someone into providing information or performing an action. It can happen through email, text messages, social media, messaging apps, or fake websites.

Example: a message claims that a school account has a problem and asks the user to click a link and sign in.

Look for unexpected messages, urgent threats, suspicious links, strange sender addresses, requests for passwords, unusual grammar, unexpected attachments, and offers that seem too good to be true.

Spear phishing is phishing specifically targeted at a person or organization. The attacker researches the target and creates a more believable message.

Example: an attacker learns that a student is part of the school robotics club and then sends a fake email pretending to be the club advisor.

Whaling is a type of targeted phishing aimed at an important or high-value individual, such as executives, school administrators, business leaders, or financial officers.

Remember: phishing is broad, spear phishing is specific, and whaling focuses on high-value targets.

Smishing is phishing through SMS or text messages. Example: “Your package could not be delivered. Confirm your information here.”

Smishing = SMS + phishing.

Vishing is phishing through voice communication, such as a phone call. An attacker may pretend to be a bank employee, technical-support employee, government employee, or school administrator.

Vishing = voice + phishing.

Pretexting occurs when an attacker creates a false story or identity to convince someone to provide information. The attacker establishes a believable pretext or reason for contacting the victim.

Example: someone calls the school technology department pretending to be a new employee who needs access to an account.

Baiting uses something appealing to trick someone into taking an unsafe action. The bait could be a free item, a fake prize, an interesting file, a tempting download, or a message promising something valuable.

Tailgating is a physical form of social engineering. An unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted area.

Example: an employee opens a secure door, and someone follows behind them without using their own access card.

AP skills: analyze risk and mitigate risk

Risk example

Asset → Student's school account

Threat → Phishing attacker

Vulnerability → Student trusts a fake login message

Impact → Attacker gains access to the account

What would you do?

You receive a message that looks like it came from your school administrator and asks you to log in to a link “immediately.”

Think about it: What is the best response?

  • Verify the request through a trusted contact method.
  • Avoid clicking suspicious links.
  • Report the message if it looks fake.
  • Use strong authentication and MFA when available.

Quick check

A student gets an email that uses details from their robotics club and pretends to be the club advisor. What is this?

Why? It is spear phishing because the attacker uses personal information to make the message look more believable and targeted.
