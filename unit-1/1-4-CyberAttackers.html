1.4 • Cyber Attacker Types

Cyber Attacker Types

Not all attackers are the same. Their skill level, goals, resources, and methods differ, and that affects the kinds of attacks they are likely to try and the defenses that matter most.

Big idea

Knowing the attacker matters.

Threat actors may be motivated by money, ideas, revenge, curiosity, disruption, political goals, or access. Their motivation helps explain what they may target and how aggressive their behavior might be.

Key attacker types

Script kiddies are relatively low-skill attackers who use tools created by other people. They may not fully understand how those tools work internally.

They are often motivated by curiosity, entertainment, recognition, or trying to cause disruption.

Hacktivists are motivated by social, political, ideological, or personal beliefs. Their attacks are often intended to express a message, protest an organization, or disrupt a target they oppose.

The key clue is the cause or belief behind the attack.

Organized criminals are motivated mainly by financial gain. They may deploy ransomware, steal credentials, steal personal information, or commit fraud.

These attacks are often more coordinated and targeted than a random prank or low-skill intrusion.

Nation states and Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) are attackers with significant resources, expertise, and patience. Their objectives may be espionage, disruption, political influence, or strategic advantage.

APT campaigns often involve long-term reconnaissance, stealth, persistence, and attempts to remain undetected.

Insider threats are people who already have legitimate access to systems, facilities, or information. This can include employees, contractors, or former employees whose access remains active.

Motivations may include revenge, greed, coercion, or helping another attacker. Insider threats are dangerous because they already have some level of trust and access.

Competitors may try to steal intellectual property, sabotage systems, or gather confidential information about rival organizations.

The goal is often strategic advantage rather than random disruption.

Hackers is a broad term for people who explore, test, or exploit technology and systems. The term may describe many kinds of attackers, including ethical hackers, developers, and malicious attackers.

It is important to remember that not all hackers are malicious. In cybersecurity, context matters.

Shadow IT refers to technology or systems used inside an organization without the approval or oversight of the official IT department. This might include unauthorized apps, file-sharing tools, personal devices, or cloud services.

Shadow IT creates risk because the organization cannot properly monitor, secure, or support those systems.

Quick comparison

  • Script kiddies: low-skill, often using tools made by others.
  • Hacktivists: driven by ideology or activism.
  • Organized criminals: driven by profit and fraud.
  • Nation states / APT: strategic, patient, highly resourced attacks.
  • Insider threats: trusted people with legitimate access.
  • Competitors: corporate or strategic intelligence attacks.
  • Hackers: broad umbrella term; context matters.
  • Shadow IT: unauthorized technology that bypasses official controls.

A government agency is targeted by a long-term campaign involving stealth, persistence, and repeated access attempts. Which attacker type is most likely involved?

Correct: This is most likely a nation state / APT actor. These attackers are typically highly resourced, patient, and focused on long-term strategic objectives.
